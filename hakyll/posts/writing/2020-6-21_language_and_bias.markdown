---
title: Practicing Inclusive Language in Code to Overcome Subconscious Bias
date: June 21, 2020
tags: language, bias, code
---

With growing awareness of recent racial injustices, tech companies are taking action. [Github](https://www.cnet.com/news/microsofts-github-is-removing-coding-terms-like-master-and-slave/) and [Golang](https://www.reddit.com/r/golang/comments/gy9ylr/go_has_removed_all_uses_of_blacklistwhitelist_and/) both recently announced removal of terms like "master/slave" and "whitelist/blacklist". Ruby on Rails made [some effort](https://github.com/rails/rails/issues/33677) towards this in 2018 and Microsoft had outlined this in their [style guide](https://docs.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/m/master-slave-master-subordinate) a while back. I have no doubt many with less prominent platforms have done so as well.

Even if you don't see the value in replacing racially charged terminology that "clearly" aren't being used with racist intent, you can at least agree that terms like "safelist" and "blocklist" are more clear than "blacklist" and "whitelist" which are one step removed from their intended meanings.

I'm glad big companies are stepping up (whatever their reasons). Precedence is important. When I tried to rename such usages at my startup in 2018, my efforts were largely met with indifference. Constantly reminding seniors and VPs that our feature was called "blocklist" and not "blacklist" was perhaps not the most productive use of anybody's time. I think any effort now to rename racist terminology will be met with much more support.

Language is neutral. It is a formation of symbols or sounds that humans use as tools to signify ideas. These ideas are anything but neutral and have layers of meanings that exist below the surface our awareness. By changing the signifier for what is intended to be signified, we also highlight its other meanings. In this moment, we can glimpse into our own subconscious biases.

I saw the potential of this when a panelist pointed out that our use of the word "but" is often unnecessary. For example, "I enjoyed dinner but the salad was rather bland". She challenged us to use "and" instead. This seemed easy enough until I tried. It was difficult and an experience I can only describe as cognitive dissonance. In our culture, we're taught not to let others. It seems at best any comment that isn't positive is "constructive criticism". Even thinking about making this switch, required confronting my own negativity and fear of disappointing others. All this was captured in a 3 letter word. This issue is dear to me. My desire to collaborate with others could not compromise with my inability to provide constructive feedback without negative judgement. This change gave me a safe opportunity to practice a better mindset. Now it's easy for me, and I find myself judging others for their performance much less. Instead, I just think how I can learn from them and better support their goals. Unlike challenging centuries of racial injustice or gender discrimination, I think few will judge you for switching to more a positive outlook. I encourage everyone to practice replacing "but" with "and".

Language is powerful. It reflects our cultural values and is shaped by our history. It encodes countless assumptions enable us to communicate incredibly complex relational messages with just a few symbols. You can find a shocking demonstration of its influence in ["A Person Paper on Purity in Language"](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/cs655/readings/purity.html) by Douglas Hofstadter (WARNING: it's clearly satire, but the racist neologism get pretty intense).

Randomly, since 2017, Microsoft Office has had a [spellchecker option to highlight biased language](https://twitter.com/garius/status/1271035621461708801). This feature was never really promoted, and it's not too late!
I love that you can choose which biases you want to work on. Change is hard and must be self driven. @garius tweet gets it right. "Your spellchecker will now help you to start overcoming your subconscious biases". I love what Microsoft is doing and hope to see others will find inspiration.

Toward this end, I've put up two bounties on gitcoin. One for building an open source dictionary of biased terms and their inclusive alternatives, another for an Atom plugin that uses this dictionary. If you'd like to see this in your favorite editor, please put up your own bounties or get coding!

Also let's not forget the power inclusive language has on its audience. It will nudge at the rusty gears in readers who are use to seeing the more familiar usage. It can be safe and subtle. Sliding in a gender neutral pronoun, for example, will unlikely be met with ire to even the most homophobic audience. Last but not least, it's a means for allies to identify each other in unsafe environments.

I knew in 2018 that asking everyone to use inclusive terminology in one (bad) feature at a failing startup would amount to absolutely nothing. That wasn't the point. I wanted to challenge myself and others to confront the bias internalized through our use of language. These things take time and visible results often don't come until many years later. We're seeing the fruits of those results now and there's a lot more work to do. Don't be afraid and don't give up.
